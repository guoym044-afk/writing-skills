# Source-derived design notes

This skill was synthesized primarily from three user-provided scanned PDFs. These notes preserve the provenance and expert basis of the workflow without requiring the original documents during routine use. The source PDFs themselves are not included in this repository.

## `Abstract1.pdf` (6 pages, Chapter 9: Abstracts)

Key source points:

- An abstract is a concise miniature of a manuscript, poster, conference presentation, or grant and often determines whether an audience reads further.
- Venue word limits and formatting rules control; major findings should be foregrounded, while unnecessary abbreviations, definitions, and citations should be avoided.
- A primary-research abstract generally includes background/relevance, a gap or need, the objective, methods, specific results, and future implications.
- A narrative review abstract generally includes background/relevance, a gap or need, an overview and synthesis of the field, and implications; it should use specific terminology rather than isolated study-level data.
- Structured abstracts expose the same rhetorical moves through headings and are common for clinical trials, medical studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses.
- A practical drafting method is to write one sentence per move, check the limit, then use remaining space for concrete methods and results in primary research or specific field terminology in a review.

## `Abstract2.pdf` (21 pages, Chapter 3: How to Write the Abstract)

Key source points:

- Journal and disciplinary instructions must be checked before drafting because abstract requirements vary.
- Summary abstracts may follow IMRaD/C or BPMRC; either can be structured or unstructured. Some venues instead prefer a result-driven abstract.
- Background, purpose/problem, methods, results, and conclusion moves form a persuasive sequence rather than a merely decorative template.
- Present tense, past tense, active voice, passive voice, personal style, and impersonal style each serve different rhetorical functions; no single choice is universally correct.
- Parallel syntax and strategic subjects can foreground a contribution, while lexical promotion must remain proportionate to evidence.
- Reusable sentence patterns can help a developing academic writer, but they must be adapted to the research rather than copied mechanically.

## `会议Abstract.pdf` (5 pages, Chapter 11: International Conferences)

Key source points:

- A conference abstract is a stand-alone selection document and may be submitted before the paper or research is complete.
- It should respond visibly to the conference theme and contain context, the research problem, methods, findings or honest research status, and the wider value of the work.
- Conference-specific instructions control length, layout, headings, abbreviations, references, tables, and figures. The source identifies 250-350 words as a common range, not a universal rule.
- A competitive conference abstract should make the topic and question clear, explain how the research was or is being conducted, summarize findings concisely, identify value and audience, and meet the exact limit.
- Extended abstracts may include references and visuals and commonly use headings such as Purpose/Aim and Background, Methodology, Findings/Results, Originality/Value, Keywords, and classification codes.

## Synthesis decisions

The skill unifies the sources into a venue-first workflow rather than imposing one fixed template. It separates primary research, narrative review, systematic/structured work, result-driven abstracts, conference abstracts, and extended abstracts. It also adds explicit evidence-integrity safeguards: never invent missing methods or results, distinguish completed from preliminary or expected findings, calibrate claims to evidence, and disclose the counting convention when the venue's own word or character counter is unavailable.
