# Abstract frameworks

This guide distills the three user-provided PDF sources into reusable writing rules. Apply venue-specific instructions before any default below.

## 1. Rule hierarchy

Apply rules in this order:

1. Current author or submission instructions from the target venue.
2. The user's explicit constraints and the paper's actual evidence.
3. Disciplinary conventions visible in relevant recent abstracts.
4. The general defaults in this guide.

Abstracts are normally self-contained miniature versions of their source work. Match the paper's order closely enough that readers can predict what the full paper contains. Draft late when possible. Respect all word, character, heading, and formatting limits.

General defaults:

- Focus on major findings rather than background detail.
- Avoid citations unless allowed.
- Avoid nonessential abbreviations and definitions beyond first use.
- Make methods and results the informational center of a primary-research abstract.
- Use specific, simple sentences and concrete terminology.

## 2. Primary-research frameworks

### Descriptive BPMRC-style framework

Use this accessible default for an original research article, poster, or oral presentation aimed beyond a narrow specialist audience.

| Move | Reader question | Required content |
|---|---|---|
| Background/relevance | Why does the topic matter? | One compact, field-anchored statement |
| Problem/gap | What is unknown or inadequate? | The precise unresolved need |
| Objective | What did this study do? | Research purpose, question, or hypothesis |
| Methods | How was it investigated? | Design, material/data, sample, key procedure, analysis |
| Results | What was found? | Principal findings with available quantitative specificity |
| Conclusion/implications | What do the findings mean? | Supported interpretation, value, or next step |

Combine adjacent moves when space is tight, such as gap + objective or result + implication. Do not omit the gap merely to add generic background. Do not turn the conclusion into a claim of universal impact.

### IMRaD/C framework

Use when the venue does not require a separate background move or when the audience already knows the context:

- Introduction/purpose
- Methods
- Results
- Discussion/conclusion

IMRaD/C and BPMRC serve the same persuasive logic: establish a worthwhile problem, show a credible approach, report informative evidence, and explain its meaning. BPMRC simply makes background and problem more explicit.

### Narrow specialist variant

A methods-and-results-heavy abstract can suit a niche audience or venue. Use it only when the audience and instructions support it. Retain enough purpose to make the method and findings interpretable.

## 3. Review frameworks

### Narrative literature review

Use:

1. Background and relevance.
2. Gap, inconsistency, or need in the field.
3. Scope and purpose of the review.
4. Synthesis of major themes, comparisons, or findings.
5. Implications, recommendations, or future direction.

Because citations are typically absent, do not present isolated study-level numbers as if they were self-evident. Prefer specific field terminology and synthesized patterns. Do not force a primary-study sample, experiment, or result structure onto a narrative review.

### Systematic review or meta-analysis

Use the venue's required structured headings. When allowed, include search scope, eligibility logic, study count, synthesis method, key pooled or synthesized findings, uncertainty, and a conclusion proportional to evidence quality.

## 4. Structured and result-driven variants

### Structured abstracts

Structured abstracts expose moves through headings instead of relying only on signal language. Reproduce mandated headings exactly. Common families include:

- Background and Aims / Methods / Key Results / Conclusions
- Objective / Methods / Results / Conclusion
- Purpose / Design-Methodology-Approach / Findings / Originality-Value

Structured format changes navigation, not the underlying content logic. It is common in clinical trials, medical studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. Do not add headings to a narrative review unless the venue requests them.

### Result-driven abstracts

Some high-selectivity or discipline-specific venues foreground results and conclusions and may greatly compress or omit background and methods. Use this form only when supported by venue instructions or a strong corpus of target-venue examples. State findings directly and keep the basis of the claim understandable.

## 5. Conference abstracts

A conference abstract is a stand-alone selection document. It may be submitted before the full paper or even before all research is complete. Compared with a journal abstract, it must work without an accompanying manuscript and should make conference relevance immediately visible.

Include:

- Research context demonstrating command of the field.
- The problem or research question.
- Methods: procedure, material or tool, data collection, and analysis as relevant.
- Findings and discussion, or a truthful statement of preliminary/expected status.
- The value of the work to the discipline and the conference audience.

Conference-specific practices:

- Name or clearly echo the conference theme when it genuinely fits.
- Treat the call for papers and submission form as binding.
- Use the specified length, layout, sections, and rules for references, abbreviations, figures, and tables.
- If no limit is available, 250-350 words is a common default from the source material, not a universal rule.
- Never convert an intended analysis into a completed result. Use wording such as “preliminary analysis indicates…” or “the study will evaluate…” only when accurate.

Conference screening questions:

1. Does the opening create informed interest without hype?
2. Are the topic and research question unmistakable?
3. Can a reviewer see how the research was or will be conducted?
4. Are actual findings summarized concretely?
5. Is the value of the work and its intended beneficiary clear?
6. Does the submission meet the exact limit and format?

## 6. Extended abstracts

An extended abstract may include references, figures, tables, images, and more methodological detail. Follow the venue template. A common structure is:

- Purpose/Aim and Background
- Methodology
- Findings/Results
- Originality/Value
- Keywords
- Classification codes when required

The source material gives two pages including references as a typical ceiling, but this is only a fallback; the venue's requirement controls. Do not add visuals unless they are permitted and materially clarify the contribution.

## 7. Language choices

Choose tense by rhetorical function rather than by a single universal rule:

| Function | Useful default |
|---|---|
| Established knowledge/current importance | Simple present |
| Prior work leading to the current gap | Present perfect or simple past |
| Objective or paper action | Simple present or simple past, following venue style |
| Completed methods | Simple past, active or passive |
| Observed results | Simple past; present when the venue treats findings as current claims |
| Interpretation and implications | Simple present or cautious modal |

Choose voice and person strategically:

- Active voice clarifies agency and often shortens sentences.
- Passive voice can foreground the object, procedure, or result and can mark an established method.
- First-person “we” can appropriately foreground a proposal, analysis, or knowledge claim when the venue permits it.
- Impersonal style can foreground the research object and suit venues that prefer it.
- Use parallel grammatical forms across method or result sentences when it improves flow.

Do not mechanically convert all prose to passive voice or remove every first-person pronoun.

## 8. Move signals and sentence patterns

Adapt these patterns; do not fill them with unsupported claims.

### Background and gap

- `[Topic] is central to [specific application or problem].`
- `Although [established progress], [precise limitation] remains unclear.`
- `Previous work has shown [known point], but has not resolved [gap].`

### Objective or contribution

- `This study investigates [question] in [scope/context].`
- `We propose [method or concept] to address [problem].`
- `The objective was to determine whether [testable question].`

### Methods

- `We analyzed [data/sample] using [design or technique].`
- `[Materials/participants] were evaluated with [procedure and analysis].`
- `The approach combines [component A] and [component B] to [purpose].`

### Results

- `The analysis showed that [finding], compared with [baseline].`
- `[Outcome] increased/decreased by [magnitude] under [condition].`
- `The proposed approach achieved [metric], exceeding [comparator] by [difference].`

### Conclusion and implications

- `These findings indicate that [narrow supported interpretation].`
- `The results support [specific use or theoretical implication] in [scope].`
- `[Method/finding] may enable [plausible application], subject to [important limitation].`

## 9. Practical space allocation

When no template controls, use this as an editing heuristic rather than a fixed rule:

- Background + gap: about 15-25%
- Objective: about 5-10%
- Methods: about 20-30%
- Results: about 30-40%
- Conclusion/implications: about 10-20%

Shift space toward results for result-driven venues and toward methods for method papers. Preserve at least one concrete principal finding in completed primary research.
