# Academic Abstract Writing

Create an accurate miniature of the paper that helps an editor, reviewer, or conference organizer quickly see the problem, approach, evidence, contribution, and relevance. Treat venue instructions as binding and the bundled PDF-derived guidance as the default when instructions are absent.

## Load the guidance

- Read [references/abstract-frameworks.md](references/abstract-frameworks.md) before drafting or restructuring an abstract. Select only the framework relevant to the user's genre.
- Read [references/revision-checklists.md](references/revision-checklists.md) when revising, compressing, translating, auditing, or finalizing an abstract.
- Read [references/source-notes.md](references/source-notes.md) only when provenance or the rationale behind the PDF-derived rules matters.

## Establish the brief

Determine, from the request and supplied material:

1. Abstract type and research status.
2. Target journal, conference, or disciplinary audience.
3. Required headings, word or character limit, language variant, keywords, and rules for citations, abbreviations, tables, or figures.
4. The paper's actual research question, methods, results, and defensible conclusion.

If venue instructions or research details are available in files or links, inspect them before writing. If a critical fact is missing, ask one focused question or use a visible placeholder such as `[sample size]`; never invent a method, number, result, novelty claim, or implication. For incomplete conference work, label preliminary findings as preliminary and expected outcomes as expected rather than presenting them as observed.

## Select the structure

Use the venue's required structure first. Otherwise choose:

- **Primary research, broad audience:** background/relevance -> gap/need -> objective -> methods -> specific results -> conclusion/implications. This is the default descriptive BPMRC-style sequence.
- **Primary research without background requirement:** purpose/introduction -> methods -> results -> discussion/conclusion (IMRaD/C).
- **Structured clinical or systematic work:** reproduce the venue's headings exactly; common headings include Background/Objectives, Methods, Results, and Conclusions.
- **Narrative literature review:** background/relevance -> gap/need -> review scope or purpose -> synthesis of major findings -> implications. Do not force primary-study methods or isolated numerical results into this genre.
- **Result-driven abstract:** foreground the principal findings and conclusions only when the venue or disciplinary model supports it.
- **Conference abstract:** align explicitly with the conference theme and make the text stand alone. Include context, problem or question, methods, findings or honest research status, and disciplinary value.
- **Extended abstract:** follow the conference template; it may include headings, keywords, references, and visuals when allowed.

Do not call a merely heading-based abstract a different content type: structured and unstructured abstracts can carry the same rhetorical moves.

## Draft from evidence

1. Build a move map using source material from the paper, notes, tables, or results.
2. Write one sentence for each required move.
3. Expand the middle with the most decision-relevant methods and concrete findings. Let methods and results occupy most of a primary-research abstract.
4. State the main result with direction, magnitude, comparison, uncertainty, or sample size when those facts are available and useful.
5. End with the narrowest implication justified by the evidence.
6. Remove content that does not help a reader decide whether the work is relevant and credible.

Draft the abstract late in the research or writing process when possible, because accurate methods and findings are then available.

## Control the language

- Prefer concise, specific, self-contained sentences and recognizable move signals.
- Use present or present perfect for established context and current knowledge gaps; use past tense for completed procedures and observations unless the venue's corpus uses another convention; use present tense or cautious modals for supported interpretations and implications.
- Choose active or passive voice according to focus and venue convention. Use first person when it clarifies agency or appropriately foregrounds a contribution; do not enforce impersonality as a universal rule.
- Define only essential nonstandard abbreviations at first use. Avoid abbreviations when they save little space.
- Avoid citations unless permitted or required. Avoid undefined jargon, empty promotion, sweeping priority claims, and claims stronger than the results.
- For primary research, use specific terms and data. For narrative reviews, synthesize patterns with field-specific terminology rather than listing uncited study-level data.
- In translation, preserve the research meaning, qualifiers, terminology, quantities, and move structure; rewrite idiomatically rather than translating word for word.

## Revise and compress

Revise in this order:

1. **Truth:** verify every claim against supplied evidence.
2. **Completeness:** confirm that required moves and headings are present.
3. **Emphasis:** protect the objective, central method, principal findings, and defensible conclusion.
4. **Coherence:** make each sentence advance one clear move and connect logically to the next.
5. **Concision:** cut generic scene-setting, repeated aims, procedural trivia, definitions, and redundant modifiers before cutting results.
6. **Compliance:** count words or characters using the venue's rules and check formatting.
7. **Polish:** fix terminology, tense, voice, grammar, punctuation, and parallelism.

Never solve a length problem by deleting the main finding or weakening factual precision.

## Deliver the result

Unless the user requests another format, return:

1. The finalized abstract in the requested language and structure.
2. The exact word count, plus character count when relevant.
3. A short note listing any placeholders, assumptions, or venue rules that still require confirmation.

When the venue's own counter is unavailable, state the counting convention used for hyphenated compounds, symbols, and units, and warn that the submission system's count controls.

When diagnosing rather than rewriting, provide a move-by-move assessment, identify unsupported or missing content, and propose prioritized fixes. When the user asks for alternatives, vary emphasis or tone without changing the underlying evidence.
