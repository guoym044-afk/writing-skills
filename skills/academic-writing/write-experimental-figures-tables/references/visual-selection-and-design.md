# Visual selection and design

## Choose by the reader's task

| Reader's task | Preferred visual | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Read exact values, precision, or many cross-comparisons | Table | Preserves numerical detail and supports comparison in several directions |
| See an overall trend or relationship | Graph/chart | Makes shape, direction, and pattern salient |
| Compare independent categories | Bar/column chart or dot plot | Highlights between-category differences |
| Follow change over ordered time, space, dose, or another continuous sequence | Line chart | Shows dependent change along an ordered independent variable |
| Examine distribution, outliers, clusters, or individual observations | Scatter, strip, box, violin, or distribution plot | Reveals variation hidden by aggregate bars |
| Show part-to-whole composition | Pie chart only for very few clear categories; otherwise a bar chart | Pie charts emphasize proportions but are difficult for close comparisons |
| Show equipment, location, or experimental layout | Diagram, schematic, map, or photograph | Communicates method or physical arrangement |
| Show process, cause-effect proposal, or workflow | Reaction scheme, flowchart, timeline, or concept map | Communicates sequence and connection |
| Compare methods or findings across studies | Structured table | Supports qualitative and quantitative synthesis with per-row citations |

Prefer the form that supports the intended claim, not the form that looks most elaborate. When uncertain, prototype more than one representation and select the clearest truthful view.

## Stand-alone requirement

Ensure the visual can be understood without reading the surrounding prose. Include, as applicable:

- experimental objective or comparison;
- conditions and essential method details;
- explicit axis, row, column, and panel labels;
- units and scale;
- definitions for colors, symbols, acronyms, and abbreviations;
- sample size and aggregation level;
- error-bar definition;
- statistical test, comparison scope, thresholds, and multiplicity handling;
- data or concept citations and reuse permissions.

## Figure design

- Place the independent variable on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis unless disciplinary convention dictates otherwise.
- Use panel letters consistently and mention every panel in the legend.
- Use color, line style, and marker shape redundantly when accessibility or grayscale reproduction matters.
- Make fonts, symbols, and line weights legible at the journal's final column width.
- When related plots must be viewed together, prefer an arrangement that remains readable after reduction; vertical stacking can be safer than cramped side-by-side panels.
- Show individual observations or distributions when they materially affect interpretation.
- Avoid encoding a quantitative claim through truncated axes or visual area in a misleading way.

## Table design

- Put the independent variable or primary comparison category in the first column.
- For method-comparison tables, consider the scientific order: objective, method, result.
- Organize like items vertically when this makes scanning easier.
- Left-align text; align numbers consistently, preferably by decimal point when required.
- Prefer a clean three-rule table without vertical lines when the target journal permits it.
- Put units in headings rather than repeating them in every cell.
- Use notes below the table for abbreviations, conditions, and statistical details; do not write a figure-style narrative caption unless the journal requires one.
- In evidence-synthesis tables, include a citation field that maps each row to its source.

## Story order

Arrange figures and tables to support the paper's reasoning, not necessarily the order in which experiments were performed. A common sequence is:

1. system, cohort, controls, or validation;
2. principal effect;
3. dose, time, subgroup, or boundary analysis;
4. robustness or alternative explanation checks;
5. mechanism, model, or integrative summary.

Use the sequence that most directly answers the stated aims or hypotheses.
