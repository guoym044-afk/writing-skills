# Language, cohesion, and paragraph conventions

## Voice and first person

- Prefer active voice when it identifies the actor and simplifies the sentence.
- Use first person for the authors' actions, findings, objectives, or paper overview when the journal permits it; do not use it to substitute personal belief for evidence.
- Use passive voice when the process or object deserves emphasis, the actor is irrelevant, or it improves old-to-new flow.
- Use past passive constructions where conventional in methods, but avoid hiding a main verb at the end of a long subject.

## Tense

- Use past tense for completed methods and results in primary research.
- Use present tense for the paper's current argument, figures/tables as displayed, and established knowledge.
- Use past tense for a cited study when its result is conditional, time/place-bound, or tied to a specific experiment.
- Use present tense for a cited result treated as established, while preserving appropriate hedging.
- Apply tense consistently within each rhetorical function and follow journal conventions.

## Claim calibration and hedging

- Use `may`, `could`, `suggest`, `indicate`, `is associated with`, or `correlates with` when causality is not established.
- Remove empty hedges that obscure a well-supported claim.
- Remove intensifiers such as `very`, `extremely`, `clearly`, and `obviously` unless they carry defined scientific meaning.
- Replace vague evaluation with evidence or a measurable criterion.

## Direct and concise sentences

- Prefer one direct verb over stacked verbs such as “performed an analysis to show.”
- Reverse unnecessary nominalizations: use “analyzed” instead of “performed an analysis of.”
- Remove throat-clearing phrases such as “it is important to note that.”
- Replace vague words such as `thing`, `a lot`, `many`, `study`, `result`, or `finding` with a specific entity, quantity, or key term when possible.
- Keep the main subject and verb close together.
- Split a sentence when it contains several independent claims, nested clauses, or a long interruption.
- Put long lists and syntactically heavy new material near the end.
- Keep list items grammatically parallel.
- Avoid contractions, direct reader address, and conversational phrasal verbs when the target register is formal.
- Prefer familiar, idiomatic academic collocations over literal Chinese-to-English translation.

Treat word-count thresholds as warnings. A sentence longer than roughly 18 words deserves inspection, but a precise, well-structured technical sentence may remain longer.

## Information flow

- Place the important subject early unless another order creates better cohesion.
- Start from old, familiar, or previously named information; end with new or complex information.
- Reuse a precise key term across related sentences before switching terms.
- Introduce the new key term in the context of the familiar term, then use it as the next sentence's starting point.
- Use pronouns and demonstratives only when the referent is unmistakable.
- Repeat a technical key term when a synonym would create ambiguity.

## Cohesion devices

Use a balanced combination of:

- reference: `this`, `these`, `such`, pronouns, and definite noun phrases;
- lexical reiteration: exact key-term repetition, controlled synonymy, superordinate terms, and domain collocations;
- conjunction: additive, adversative, causal, and temporal relations;
- syntactic linkage: subordination, comparison, parallelism, and purposeful sentence combination.

Do not begin every sentence with a connector. Make the semantic relation clear first, then add a transition only when the reader needs an explicit signal.

## Paragraph audit

- Identify the single main job of the paragraph.
- Check that the topic sentence and key terms predict its content.
- Ensure every sentence relates to the paragraph's main claim.
- Arrange evidence in a defensible order: general-to-specific, known-to-unknown, cause-to-effect, chronological, problem-to-solution, or objective-method-result.
- Place analysis after the evidence it interprets.
- Replace summary-only final sentences with analysis, implication, qualification, or a useful transition—or delete them.
- Check the last sentence of one paragraph and the first sentence of the next for a visible logical handoff.
