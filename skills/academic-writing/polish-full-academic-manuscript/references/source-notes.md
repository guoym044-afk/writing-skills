# Source-derived design notes

This skill was synthesized primarily from two user-provided scanned PDFs. These notes preserve the provenance and expert basis of the workflow without requiring the source documents during routine use. The source PDFs and OCR intermediates are not included in this repository.

## `对全文进行语言、衔接、段落和整体结构修改.pdf` (24 pages)

Key source points:

- Scientific revision is critical rereading and rewriting, not merely editing surface errors or proofreading.
- Revision should move from global concerns to local ones: purpose, contribution and audience; ideas and evidence; organization; clarity and style; grammar, punctuation and spelling; and final proofreading.
- IMRaD/AIMRaD provides a common functional structure, but target-journal and disciplinary requirements control.
- General-to-specific and situation-problem-solution-evaluation patterns help organize scientific arguments.
- Cohesion depends on grammatical reference, conjunction, lexical repetition and collocation; transitions should reveal real relationships rather than decorate prose.
- Effective scientific writing should be accurate, brief and clear, with precise vocabulary, idiomatic collocations, controlled sentence length, parallel structures and the important subject placed early.
- Old information should normally precede new information so that each sentence grows from what the reader already knows.
- Major claims require relevant evidence and interpretation; results should not be left to “speak for themselves.”
- A strong paragraph uses a recognizable topic or key sentence, supporting evidence or reasoning, and a conclusion or analysis when it adds value.

## `对全文进行语言和段落修改.pdf` (13 pages, Chapter 7: Style conventions)

Key source points:

- Scientific style aims to inform through concise, specific and formal prose rather than entertain through decorative language.
- Active voice and first person can improve clarity and appropriately foreground an author's action or finding; passive voice remains useful in methods and when it supports information flow.
- Past tense generally reports completed methods and results. Present or past tense can frame cited research differently depending on whether a result is treated as established or context-bound.
- Correlational evidence requires calibrated language such as `may`, `suggest`, `correlates with` or `is associated with` instead of unsupported causation.
- Direct verbs are preferable to stacked verbs and unnecessary nominalizations.
- Familiar, specific key terms belong near sentence beginnings; new, complex information and long lists generally belong later.
- Paragraph flow improves when a key term is repeated across related sentences, a new term is introduced in the context of that familiar term, and the new term then becomes familiar.
- A common evidence-based paragraph moves from a topic sentence through detailed evidence to concise analysis, implication, limitation or comparison.
- Sentence and paragraph revision should be recursive, with a few explicit style goals addressed at a time.

## Synthesis decisions

The skill combines both sources into a six-pass global-to-local workflow and a persistent manuscript map for long documents. It preserves their expert rules while treating mechanical thresholds as diagnostics: the source's guidance to inspect sentences over roughly 18 words does not become a hard ceiling, and passive voice, first person, transitions and paragraph endings are selected according to clarity, evidence, venue and disciplinary convention. The skill also adds explicit integrity safeguards so that polishing cannot alter data, citations, equations, technical meaning or the warranted strength of a claim.
