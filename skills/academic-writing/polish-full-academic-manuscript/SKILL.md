# Polish Full Academic Manuscript

## Core mandate

Revise from global meaning to local wording. Treat polishing as critical rereading and rewriting, not synonym replacement or grammar correction alone. Improve the manuscript's communicative effectiveness without inventing evidence, strengthening claims beyond the data, changing technical meaning, or imposing ornamental prose.

Use the principles distilled from the two user-provided scientific-writing PDFs. Read:

## Establish the editing contract

Identify the manuscript language, discipline, document type, target journal or style guide, intended audience, desired depth, and requested output. Infer safe defaults when these are evident. If no journal guidance is available, use contemporary scientific English conventions and label journal-dependent choices.

Distinguish three levels:

1. **Language edit**: Correct grammar, diction, concision, syntax, terminology, and local cohesion.
2. **Substantive edit**: Also reorganize sentences and paragraphs, improve topic progression, and strengthen section transitions.
3. **Structural edit**: Also diagnose purpose, contribution, claim-evidence alignment, section order, repetition, omissions, and IMRaD/AIMRaD coherence.

For a request concerning the full manuscript, default to structural diagnosis followed by substantive and language editing. Do not silently rewrite uncertain technical content. Mark it with `[AUTHOR CHECK: ...]` or an equivalent comment and explain the uncertainty.

## Preserve invariants

Preserve all verified facts, numerical values, units, statistical results, equations, variable names, citations, reference keys, figure/table identifiers, technical terms, and stated limitations unless the user authorizes content changes. Preserve calibrated uncertainty: do not turn correlation into causation, possibility into certainty, or a limited result into a universal claim.

Do not fabricate citations, methods, findings, contributions, limitations, or journal rules. Cross-check apparent contradictions before editing them. If a sentence is scientifically ambiguous, offer the most likely revision and flag the point instead of guessing.

## Build a manuscript map before rewriting

For a complete paper, first extract a compact map containing:

- the central research question or problem;
- the claimed novelty and contributions;
- the evidence supporting each major claim;
- the function of every section and major paragraph;
- repeated technical terms, abbreviations, tense/voice conventions, and notation;
- gaps, contradictions, misplaced material, and duplicated claims.

Use this map as a consistency ledger throughout the edit. For long manuscripts, edit in batches while retaining the map, terminology sheet, unresolved-author-query list, and decisions from earlier batches.

## Apply the six-pass workflow

Work in this order; do not begin with line editing when global revisions may make that work obsolete.

1. **Purpose, contribution, and audience**: Verify that the title, abstract, final introduction paragraph, discussion, and conclusion express compatible aims and contributions at an appropriate level of specificity.
2. **Ideas, evidence, and claims**: Check that methods answer the research question, results provide the promised evidence, interpretations follow from the results, and limitations or counterevidence are handled. Replace “results speak for themselves” logic with concise analysis.
3. **Overall organization**: Check target-journal requirements and use IMRaD/AIMRaD as a functional model, not a rigid template. Reorder content when necessary. Use general-to-specific or situation-problem-solution-evaluation patterns where they clarify the argument.
4. **Paragraphs and transitions**: Give each paragraph one primary job. Use a topic/key-term sentence, connected evidence or reasoning, and analysis/implication when warranted. Connect paragraphs through logical progression and lexical continuity rather than a surplus of transition words.
5. **Sentences and academic style**: Prefer precise subjects, direct verbs, familiar/old information before new information, parallel lists, concise syntax, and calibrated voice and tense. Split sentences that carry multiple independent reasoning steps. Retain necessary complexity.
6. **Mechanics and consistency**: Correct grammar, articles, agreement, number, punctuation, spelling, capitalization, abbreviations, terminology, notation, citations, figure/table references, and formatting consistency. Perform this pass after substantive changes.

## Revise transparently

When changes are nontrivial, distinguish:

- **Correction**: fixes an objective language or consistency error;
- **Improvement**: enhances clarity, concision, cohesion, or emphasis without changing meaning;
- **Author decision**: requires domain knowledge, new evidence, or a judgment about intended meaning.

Explain representative high-impact changes, not every article or comma. Show before/after text only when it helps the author learn or approve a consequential change.

## Use sentence metrics as diagnostics, not laws

Treat long sentences, passive voice, nominalization, first person, and transition frequency as signals for inspection. Do not mechanically enforce an 18-word ceiling or convert every passive construction to active voice. Prefer the form that makes the actor, action, information flow, and disciplinary convention clearest. Follow explicit journal rules over defaults in this skill.

## Complete the final audit

After rewriting, reread the manuscript at three scales:

1. Read headings, first sentences, and final sentences to test the argument spine.
2. Read paragraph by paragraph to test topic unity, key-term continuity, evidence, and analysis.
3. Read sentence by sentence to test meaning, grammar, terminology, citations, and mechanics.

Then reconcile the revised manuscript with the manuscript map. Report unresolved author checks and any edits deliberately withheld. Never claim that the full manuscript has been checked if only an excerpt was available.
