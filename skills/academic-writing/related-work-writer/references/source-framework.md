## Source A: Review Structure and Binning

**PDF:** `学习Related Work如何分类、比较和组织.pdf` (6 pages)

- A review introduction moves from topic relevance and definitions to a field need or gap and then to a roadmap (pp. 1-2).
- Main-text research may be organized methodologically, thematically, or chronologically. Sections normally contain multiple paragraphs and compare several cited sources; tables can support collective comparison (p. 3).
- A review conclusion synthesizes findings, identifies a specific field need, and proposes future work without adding new information (p. 4).
- The binning method starts from an overarching question, previews recent sources, gives each source short labels based on recurring language, findings, methods, or themes, and groups sources into potential sections (pp. 4-5).
- Bins may overlap. Roughly 2-5 bins are usually more workable than one very broad bin or many shallow bins. Sparse bins can motivate further searching; sources that fit no bin may be irrelevant (p. 6).
- Logical ordering options include problem-solution, broad-specific, known-unknown, cause-effect, process steps, and objective-method-result. Older or established knowledge can precede newer work with greater uncertainty (p. 6).

## Source B: Micro-Organization, Cohesion, and Citations

**PDF:** `学习Related Work如何回顾、比较、组织并导向研究缺口（辅助）.pdf` (10 pages)

- General-specific organization moves from a broad statement to increasingly specific information; an ending sentence may widen the implication again (pp. 1-2).
- Problem-solution organization uses situation, problem, solution, and evaluation. In Related Work, this can organize a research stream or explain why a class of methods emerged (p. 2).
- Cohesion relies on grammatical reference, conjunction, and lexical cohesion. Demonstratives, comparison terms, repeated key words, and additive/adversative/causal/temporal links make relationships explicit (pp. 3-6).
- Correct sentences can still produce poor flow when the relationship between ideas is implicit. Use connectors, reference terms, and controlled repetition to make the relation recoverable (pp. 5-7).
- Citations support arguments and require movement between the writer's synthesis and source-based summary or paraphrase. Follow the disciplinary or venue citation system (pp. 7-10).
- Select significant, closely relevant references and verify every bibliographic element against the original publication. Cross-check in-text citations and the reference list (p. 10).

## Source C: Prior Research, Limitations, Gaps, and Positioning

**PDF:** `学习Related Work如何回顾、比较、组织并导向研究缺口.pdf` (15 pages)

- A strong introduction establishes field importance, describes previous/current research and contributions, locates a gap by identifying limitations or the problem to address, and describes the present paper (p. 1).
- A concise example moves from general background to specific prior findings, contribution, absence or limitation, and the current research purpose. Repetition, adversative conjunction, and reference expressions create cohesion (pp. 2-4).
- A multi-paragraph example separates general background, research objective, prior contributions, multiple limitations, and the present method/contribution. `However` marks contrast and a causal transition connects limitations to the current purpose (pp. 4-7).
- A longer example narrows from the broad field to materials, specific approaches, their measured contributions, limitations, theoretical requirements, and the current study. Topic sentences and specific evidence sustain the broad-to-specific structure (pp. 8-14).
- Writers should inspect target-journal instructions and highly regarded papers to learn macro structure, component length, linking devices, disciplinary expressions, and syntactic patterns. Imitate patterns rather than copying sentences (p. 15).

## Combined Implications for Related Work

1. Start with the current paper's comparison need, not author chronology.
2. Use binning to reveal the field's structure before writing headings.
3. Choose one dominant organization and make section order argumentative.
4. Synthesize prior contributions before identifying their shared or contrasting boundaries.
5. Derive the gap from those boundaries, state why it matters, and position the present work proportionally.
6. Use topic sentences, stable key terms, references, and explicit logical links to maintain flow.
7. End without introducing unsupported claims or new literature.
