# Audit Academic Citations

## Purpose

Determine whether each source-dependent claim is cited, whether the cited source actually supports it, whether credit is assigned to the correct source, and whether borrowed language has been transformed ethically. Separate content accuracy from citation style: a perfectly formatted citation can still be unsupported or misattributed.

## Required references

- Read [references/source-guidance.md](references/source-guidance.md) before auditing source use, attribution, quotation, summary, or paraphrase.
- Read [references/audit-rubric.md](references/audit-rubric.md) before grading findings or producing an audit report.

## Establish the evidence set

1. Inventory the manuscript, reference list, target style or journal guide, and available source documents.
2. Distinguish full sources from abstracts, snippets, reviews, bibliographies, and metadata-only records.
3. Treat the original full text as the authority for support and attribution. Do not infer support from a title, citation count, search snippet, or reference entry.
4. If the original source is unavailable, mark claim support or attribution as `UNVERIFIABLE`; do not guess.
5. Ask for missing originals only when their absence prevents the requested conclusion. Continue with citation placement, reference consistency, and wording checks that remain possible.

Use the PDF, document, or spreadsheet skill appropriate to the supplied files. For scanned PDFs, perform OCR and visually verify passages that determine a finding.

## Choose audit depth

- **Screening:** Find likely missing, misplaced, inconsistent, or suspicious citations without asserting source support.
- **Claim-to-source audit:** Compare each cited claim with the relevant original passage, table, figure, method, or result.
- **Attribution audit:** Trace whether the cited source produced the information or merely cited another source.
- **Language-use audit:** Compare manuscript wording with sources for quotation, patchwriting, sentence-mining, and adequate paraphrase.

If the user does not specify depth, perform the deepest audit supported by the available evidence and state the limitation.

## Build claim units

Audit at the smallest meaningful unit: a clause, sentence, list item, table cell, figure caption, or quantitative statement. Split compound sentences when different clauses require different evidence.

For every unit, record:

- stable location such as page, section, paragraph, sentence, table, or figure;
- exact or tightly summarized claim;
- attached citation or absence of one;
- whether the content is source-dependent, common knowledge, or the author's own new work/analysis;
- required evidence type: primary experiment, review/background synthesis, method, dataset, standard, theory, or other;
- available supporting passage, page, figure, table, or section.

Do not treat a citation at the end of a long paragraph as automatically supporting all preceding sentences.

## Run the six checks

### 1. Citation necessity

Require citations for borrowed methods or data, specific facts, background claims, broad conclusions or analyses from sources, and field-specific knowledge under active research. Do not require citations for the author's unpublished objective, methods, results, or clearly signposted original analysis. When uncertain, favor citation while avoiding irrelevant citation padding.

### 2. Placement and scope

Place a citation where its supported claim ends. Repeat it in consecutive sentences if both use the source. Split citations within a sentence when sources support different clauses or list items. Keep the boundary between cited evidence and the author's inference explicit.

### 3. Claim support

Compare claim strength, population, conditions, direction, magnitude, modality, and certainty with the source. A source supports a claim only when the relevant passage or data entails it without material expansion. Flag citation presence without evidentiary support.

### 4. Correct attribution

Identify who produced the evidence. For a primary article, attribute only the study's own objective, methods, results, and appropriately bounded conclusions. Do not attribute a statement from its introduction or discussion to that article when the statement is credited there to another work. Use secondary sources for broad background or synthesis; avoid using a review as if it were the primary producer of exact experimental data unless the wording transparently reports a review-level synthesis.

### 5. Ethical source language

Classify use as direct quotation, acceptable summary/paraphrase, patchwriting, sentence-mining, or likely verbatim copying. Do not equate synonym substitution with paraphrase. Compare idea selection, sentence structure, information order, and distinctive wording. In scientific writing, prefer concise synthesis over quotation unless the discipline or task requires exact language. If quoting, preserve exact wording, quotation marks or block format, citation, and page/location required by the style.

### 6. Reference and style integrity

Check every in-text citation against the reference list and every listed reference against the text. Verify author, title, year, venue, volume/issue, pages, DOI or URL, and publication status against the original record when available. Apply the target journal or style guide; do not mix citation-order, author-year, and alphabet-number conventions. Treat style errors as separate from support and attribution errors.

## Revise only from verified evidence

When asked to fix problems:

1. Preserve the supported scientific meaning, scope, and certainty.
2. Narrow or qualify overstated claims before adding citations.
3. Move or split citations to show exact support boundaries.
4. Rewrite from understanding or notes, not by editing the source sentence word by word.
5. Cite the original evidence producer when available. If only a secondary source was consulted, make the indirect nature transparent and follow the target style.
6. Never invent a source, page number, quotation, DOI, finding, or bibliographic field.
7. Show substantive revisions and explain why they solve the finding.

## Deliver the audit

Lead with an overall verdict and evidence limitations. Then provide:

1. a prioritized finding table using the rubric codes;
2. claim-level evidence with manuscript location and source location;
3. recommended corrections, clearly separated from verified facts;
4. a short coverage summary: units checked, sources available, sources missing, and items still unverifiable;
5. corrected text only when requested or when a concise example is essential.

Use the manuscript's language unless the user requests another. Avoid declaring plagiarism as a legal or disciplinary fact; report observable textual overlap and plagiarism risk.

## Stop conditions

Do not issue a definitive `PASS` for claim support or attribution when the original source was not inspected. Do not silently replace an unsupported citation with a plausible one. Do not remove citations merely to improve prose. Do not let citation formatting conceal uncertainty about evidence.
