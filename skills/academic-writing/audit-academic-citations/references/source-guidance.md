## 1. Evidence and documentation

Scientific arguments require evidence. Documentation lets readers locate sources, verify data, distinguish borrowed material from the writer's contribution, and follow connections across research. Writers must move clearly between their own reasoning and material used as quotation, summary, or paraphrase.

Treat these as separate questions:

- Is a citation needed?
- Is it placed at the correct claim boundary?
- Does the source support the claim?
- Did the cited authors originate the evidence?
- Is the source language used ethically?
- Does the citation and reference metadata follow the outlet's style?

## 2. Source selection

Prefer significant, published, directly relevant sources. Accepted papers may be marked `in press` or `forthcoming` when the outlet permits it.

Use source types according to their evidentiary role:

- **Primary research:** cite the study's objective, method, result, or bounded conclusion. Use it as specific evidence.
- **Review or secondary source:** cite background, well-established concepts, broad conclusions, or synthesis. Use it to connect readers to a larger body of research.
- **Preprint or organizational report:** identify its status and assess authority for the claim.
- **News, general websites, and textbooks:** normally avoid for research evidence when a scientific or authoritative original source is available.

Do not confuse relevance with support. A paper on the same topic may not support the exact claim.

## 3. Citation necessity

Normally cite:

- specific methods and data from other sources;
- quantitative values, thresholds, dates, classifications, or definitions that are not the author's;
- broad conclusions, concepts, models, or analyses from other sources;
- background information, especially topics under active research;
- claims about what prior researchers found, proposed, argued, or failed to address;
- adapted tables, figures, instruments, code, or datasets.

Normally do not cite:

- the objective, methods, and results of the author's own unpublished study;
- clearly signposted original analysis or interpretation;
- topic and transition sentences that contain no borrowed factual claim;
- genuine common knowledge that is well established across the intended discipline.

Use `common knowledge` cautiously. Knowledge familiar to the writer may still be specialized or actively researched. If doubt remains, cite an appropriate recent authoritative source.

## 4. Citation placement

- Put the citation at the end of the claim it supports, usually before punctuation unless the outlet specifies otherwise.
- Cite a source each time a sentence uses it, even in consecutive sentences.
- Put multiple citations together when they contribute equally to the same claim.
- Split citations when they support different clauses, data points, list items, or parts of a sentence.
- Separate cited research from the writer's new result or analysis with wording and placement.
- If using an author-integral construction such as `X et al. found...`, place the year or numerical marker directly with the author name as required by the style.
- Do not make readers infer whether one citation supports a sentence, several sentences, or a whole paragraph.

## 5. Attribution and source lineage

Incorrect attribution occurs when credit is assigned to the wrong researchers or source type.

### Primary-source test

Before attributing a finding to a primary article, locate it in the article's own methods, results, figures, or tables. If the relevant sentence in the article cites another work, the current article may be a relay source rather than the origin.

Common high-risk locations are abstracts, introductions, and discussion background. These sections often combine the study's own claims with cited prior research. Do not attribute everything on the first page to the paper's authors.

### Secondary-source test

Use a review for its own synthesis or for broad background. Do not present an exact experimental result from a review as though the reviewers conducted that experiment. If the original study is unavailable and an indirect citation is permitted, state the indirect relationship transparently.

### Attribution boundary test

For each clause, ask:

1. Who generated the data or idea?
2. Where in the source is that ownership shown?
3. Is the manuscript reporting the source's evidence, the source's interpretation, another cited study, or the manuscript author's inference?
4. Does the reporting verb match the evidence and certainty (`found`, `reported`, `suggested`, `argued`, `reviewed`, `estimated`)?

## 6. Quotation, summary, and paraphrase

### Direct quotation

Direct quotation repeats exact language. Mark it visibly and give the locator required by the style. In scientific writing, prefer concise paraphrase or synthesis because the focus is usually evidence rather than memorable phrasing. Do not permit unmarked verbatim language merely because a citation is present.

### Summary

A summary digests a meaningful chunk of source material, selects task-relevant information, and condenses it into one or two sentences or less. It must be shorter than the source and demonstrate understanding rather than reproduce the source sentence order.

For primary research, summarize the study's own methods or results. For reviews, summarize concepts or background within the review's synthesis.

### Paraphrase

Paraphrase communicates the source's meaning in an independently constructed expression. It may retain necessary technical terminology and exact data, but should not mirror the source's syntax, information sequence, or distinctive phrasing.

### Error patterns

- **Verbatim copying/plagiarism risk:** identical or near-identical language without valid quotation treatment; a citation alone does not cure it.
- **Patchwriting:** source syntax and information order remain while words are swapped, deleted, or lightly rearranged.
- **Sentence-mining:** the writer rewrites one source sentence rather than understanding and synthesizing a larger unit.
- **Incorrect attribution:** the wording may be original, but the cited source did not produce the stated evidence.

Assess language overlap separately from factual support. An original paraphrase can still misrepresent a source; an accurate statement can still be copied improperly.

## 7. Active-reading workflow

Read for the task rather than mechanically from beginning to end.

1. Pre-read the title and abstract.
2. Identify article type, research gap, objective or hypothesis, and overall findings.
3. Read background only as needed for comprehension.
4. Deep-read relevant results, figures, tables, methods, and controls.
5. Compare figures and tables with the written results.
6. Check whether the results match the title, objective, conclusions, and claimed certainty.
7. Note limitations and the boundary between prior research and the current study.
8. Close or hide the source and draft from notes or understanding.
9. Reopen the source to verify terminology, numbers, scope, and attribution.

Do not paraphrase while staring at the source sentence. Do not replace words with synonyms as a writing method.

## 8. Figure Facts workflow

Use this method when figures or tables are central and interpretable.

For each figure or table, record:

1. known background needed to interpret it;
2. the experiment or analysis question;
3. the method;
4. the observed result, including direction, magnitude, uncertainty, and conditions;
5. how the visual compares with the authors' written result;
6. which observation is relevant to the manuscript's task.

Draft a cited sentence from this analysis, then add verified technical terms or data. This reduces sentence-mining and helps identify claims independently. If the visual cannot stand alone or the method is unfamiliar, read the result and method alongside it.

## 9. Citation systems and reference integrity

Common systems include:

- citation order: references numbered by first appearance;
- author-year: author name and publication year in text;
- alphabet-number: reference list numbered alphabetically, so in-text numbers need not appear sequentially.

These are conventions, not evidence rules. The target journal's instructions supersede general patterns, including punctuation placement, author truncation, page locators, brackets, superscripts, and ordering.

Before submission:

- match every in-text citation to exactly one reference-list entry;
- find and resolve every uncited reference-list entry;
- check all bibliographic elements against the original publication or authoritative record;
- verify page or table locators for direct quotations and specifically located material;
- keep one citation system consistently unless the outlet explicitly requires otherwise.
