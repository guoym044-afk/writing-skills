# Map IMRaD Paper Structure

## Objective

Turn notes, an outline, or a manuscript into one traceable scientific argument:

`field context -> gap or need -> objective/question -> method -> result -> interpretation -> contribution`

Prioritize the paper's functional logic over conventional section names. Treat IMRaD as a set of required information functions even when a journal merges, renames, reorders, or omits familiar headings.

## Load the knowledge basis

Read [references/imrad-logic.md](references/imrad-logic.md) completely before planning or auditing a paper. Use its section moves, alignment rules, and diagnostic checks as the knowledge basis for this workflow.

## Select the operating mode

- **Plan**: Build a full-paper storyline and section blueprint from a research idea, evidence set, or experiment plan.
- **Audit**: Recover the current storyline from a manuscript and locate broken, missing, duplicated, or unsupported links.
- **Restructure**: Recommend a new section, subsection, paragraph, or figure order while preserving the scientific meaning.
- **Explain**: Clarify the role of a section or the relationship between sections without redesigning the whole paper.

If the user does not name a mode, infer it from the material and state the selected mode in one short phrase.

## Workflow

### 1. Establish constraints

Identify the paper type, target journal or venue, development stage, central claim, research questions or hypotheses, available methods/data, and intended audience. Consult the target journal's current author instructions when they are supplied or when web access is authorized and journal-specific compliance is requested.

Do not block on missing context. Mark consequential assumptions and unresolved decisions explicitly.

### 2. State the four-question backbone

Express the paper in one sentence for each question:

1. What problem or question was studied, and why does it matter?
2. How was the problem studied?
3. What was found?
4. What do the findings mean?

Reject vague answers that cannot be connected to concrete evidence.

### 3. Build the alignment matrix

Create one row per research question, hypothesis, objective, or claim. Use these columns:

| ID | Gap or need | Objective / RQ / hypothesis | Method and data | Result or evidence | Discussion meaning | Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Enforce bidirectional traceability:

- Give every objective a method, result, and discussion-level answer.
- Give every major method a stated purpose.
- Give every major result a question or objective that it answers.
- Give every discussion claim direct support in the results.
- Connect every claimed contribution to the original gap or need.

Label an empty or uncertain cell as `missing`, `unclear`, or `unsupported`; never invent the missing science.

### 4. Map the macro shape

Apply the hourglass logic:

- Narrow the Introduction from field context to the precise gap and present study.
- Keep Methods and Results at the most specific and technical level.
- Widen the Discussion from the paper's findings to their field-level meaning, limitations, and future work.

Map functions rather than headings when the paper uses nonstandard organization. Verify the target journal before treating a conventional order as mandatory.

### 5. Design or recover section moves

Use the moves in the knowledge basis to determine what each section must accomplish. For every section or subsection, state:

- its governing question;
- its required content and evidence;
- the order of its moves;
- the transition into the next section;
- the alignment-matrix rows it serves.

For Results, plan figures and tables before prose when possible. Order evidence by argumentative buildup toward the main result, not automatically by experimental chronology.

### 6. Check micro-organization

Inspect representative paragraphs and section transitions for:

- general-to-specific progression, with optional widening at the end;
- situation -> problem -> solution -> evaluation where problem-solution logic is needed;
- explicit logical relations through reference, comparison, cause, contrast, sequence, and controlled repetition of key terms;
- stable terminology across the gap, objective, results, and discussion.

Do not treat more transition words as inherently better. Require each connective to express a real logical relation.

### 7. Diagnose structural defects

Separate defects into four classes:

- **Storyline**: unclear gap, competing central questions, or contribution disconnected from the problem.
- **Alignment**: orphan methods/results, unanswered objectives, unsupported discussion claims, or mismatched terminology.
- **Sequence**: moves, subsections, figures, or paragraphs appear in an order that weakens comprehension.
- **Cohesion**: adjacent claims lack an explicit semantic connection or stable referent.

Prioritize issues as:

- `P0`: invalidates or obscures the central claim;
- `P1`: breaks a major cross-section link;
- `P2`: weakens local flow without changing the scientific argument.

### 8. Deliver a decision-ready structure

Return, in this order:

1. **Structural verdict**: one short assessment of whether the full storyline currently works.
2. **One-sentence backbone**: the complete problem-to-contribution chain.
3. **Four-question map**: concise answers to the IMRaD backbone.
4. **Alignment matrix**: one row per objective, RQ, hypothesis, or claim.
5. **Section blueprint**: recommended section/subsection order and the job of each part.
6. **Evidence order**: recommended figure/table sequence when relevant.
7. **Priority issues**: P0, P1, and P2 findings with concrete locations when a manuscript is supplied.
8. **Decisions and unknowns**: what is fixed, what is assumed, and what evidence or author choice remains necessary.
9. **Next revision actions**: a short ordered list of changes.

Prefer one recommended structure. Offer alternatives only when a genuine scientific or venue-dependent choice remains.

## Integrity rules

- Preserve the distinction between observed content, inference, and recommendation.
- Do not manufacture data, results, citations, novelty, limitations, or contributions.
- Do not force a paper into identical section titles when its field or target journal uses a valid variation.
- Do not solve sentence-level style problems before the macro storyline and cross-section alignment are sound.
- Cite page, section, subsection, paragraph, figure, or table locations when auditing supplied material.
