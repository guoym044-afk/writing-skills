## Core purpose

Use the Introduction to provide the rationale for the paper and attract informed interest. Move from a broad but relevant discussion of the topic to the precise question investigated. The section should enable a reader to identify:

- why the research area matters;
- what is already known and how it is known;
- what remains unresolved and why that matters;
- what the present study does about it.

Length, components, and order vary across disciplines and venues. Start with the target venue, then use the model below as a reasoning scaffold.

## Flexible move model

### Move 1: Establish the territory

State the research field, its relevance, the central phenomenon, or a necessary definition. A statistic may be useful when it is authoritative, current, and directly relevant; it is not mandatory.

Typical functions:

- define a key concept;
- establish scope and significance;
- state a well-supported fact or practical/theoretical concern;
- introduce the specific system, population, material, or setting.

Avoid generic claims such as “X has attracted increasing attention” unless evidence and relevance make the statement informative.

### Move 2: Synthesize prior research

Orient the reader with only the literature needed to reach the niche. Proceed from general knowledge to the most pertinent methods, findings, mechanisms, or competing explanations.

Organize by theme, approach, agreement, contrast, or limitation. Make the relationship among sources explicit. Use reviews for broad orientation and primary studies for specific findings and data.

### Move 3: Establish the niche

Identify a defensible reason for the present study:

- a missing population, condition, scale, mechanism, comparison, or validation;
- a limitation in data, method, theory, measurement, or generalizability;
- conflicting or inconclusive findings;
- a practical need not met by current approaches;
- an unanswered research question.

A gap is not merely “few studies exist.” Explain what is unknown, why existing work cannot answer it, and what consequence follows. Cite the literature that supports the gap whenever possible.

### Move 4: Occupy the niche

State the study's response to the niche. Depending on venue conventions, include one or more of:

- objective or purpose;
- research question or testable hypothesis;
- study scope and design;
- reason for choosing a method;
- principal finding or contribution;
- significance or application;
- paper organization.

The objective must answer the gap at the same level of specificity. Do not introduce a broader promise than the study actually tests.

## Paragraph architectures

### Short primary-research Introduction

1. Field and relevance; essential known facts.
2. Most pertinent prior work; limitation or unresolved issue.
3. Explicit gap/need, objective, and optionally design, hypothesis, or principal contribution.

### Standard multi-paragraph Introduction

1. Broad field, relevance, and core concept.
2. Current state of knowledge and major approaches.
3. More specific evidence, tensions, or limitations.
4. Verified gap and its consequence.
5. Present objective/question/hypothesis, study design, and optional contribution or roadmap.

### Detailed or review-oriented Introduction

Use additional paragraphs only when each has a distinct role. Continue narrowing from the field to the subproblem; give each paragraph a topic sentence and evidence; reserve the final segment for the niche and present response.

## Gap-to-objective logic

Test the logic as four linked statements:

1. **Known:** Evidence establishes A.
2. **Unresolved:** Existing work does not establish B under condition C.
3. **Need:** Without B, consequence D remains unresolved or practice E is impaired.
4. **Response:** This study evaluates/develops/tests B under C using method F.

If statement 4 does not directly answer statement 2, revise either the gap or objective. If statement 3 is weak, the study rationale will feel unimportant even when the gap is real.

## Literature synthesis

Create a synthesis table before drafting when the source bundle is large:

| Theme or claim | Sources | Agreement | Difference/limitation | Role in Introduction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Established knowledge | Review + primary studies | What is consistent | Boundary conditions | Territory |
| Current approaches | Primary studies | Shared method/result | Trade-offs | Prior research |
| Unresolved issue | Most direct sources | What is known | Exact missing evidence | Niche |

Convert the table into prose organized by ideas, not a sequence of author summaries.

## Optional elements

Include only when supported by the venue and article type:

- **Definitions:** when terminology is ambiguous or specialized.
- **Brief methods:** when method choice is central to how the study fills the gap.
- **Principal results:** common in some concise or high-impact formats, absent in others.
- **Significance/contribution:** useful when specific and non-promotional.
- **Paper roadmap:** more common in long articles and reviews; often unnecessary in short reports.

## Common failure modes

- Opening with a field-wide claim that does not lead to the study.
- Treating the literature review as a catalogue of papers.
- Claiming “no studies” without a systematic basis.
- Placing the objective before the reader understands the need.
- Naming a method without explaining how it addresses the gap.
- Adding results or a roadmap simply because a template includes them.
- Using excessive background, citations, or terminology unrelated to the niche.
- Equating novelty with significance.
