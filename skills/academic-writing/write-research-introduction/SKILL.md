# Write Research Introduction

Build an Introduction that gives the study a defensible rationale, guides the reader from the field to the exact research need, and makes the present study easy to identify. Preserve disciplinary accuracy and never manufacture evidence.

## Route the request

Choose the nearest mode:

- **Plan**: turn a research brief and sources into a move map and paragraph outline.
- **Draft**: write a new Introduction from verified claims, citations, and study details.
- **Revise**: diagnose an existing draft, then restructure or rewrite it.
- **Polish**: improve clarity, cohesion, concision, tense, and academic tone without changing claims.
- **Audit**: evaluate move coverage, gap logic, evidence, citations, journal fit, and language.
- **Translate**: reconstruct a publication-quality English Introduction from Chinese; translate meaning and rhetorical function, not sentence order.

When the user asks to “敲定/finalize,” perform **Revise + Polish + Audit** and return a ready-to-use version plus unresolved evidence risks.

## Establish the working brief

Collect or infer:

1. article type and discipline;
2. target journal/conference and word or citation style constraints;
3. research problem, scope, and key terms;
4. what prior work establishes and what remains unresolved;
5. the exact objective, research question, or hypothesis;
6. method/design and principal result or contribution, if appropriate for the venue;
7. verified sources for every literature or factual claim.

Do not block on every missing field. If the study is still underspecified, produce a move map or a draft with explicit tags such as `[EVIDENCE NEEDED]`, `[GAP TO VERIFY]`, and `[STUDY OBJECTIVE]`. In an early working draft, keep tags brief and place them after the affected sentence or paragraph; also consolidate them under **Evidence flags**. Remove all tags from a final draft only after the underlying issue is resolved. Ask only for information that would materially change the scientific claim.

## Follow the workflow

### 1. Calibrate to the venue

Read the target venue's current author instructions when available. Examine recent or highly regarded papers from the same venue and field to infer expected length, paragraph count, placement of hypotheses, use of first person, citation density, and whether brief methods, findings, contributions, or a paper roadmap belong in the Introduction.

Treat venue conventions as higher priority than a generic template. When no venue is supplied, use a neutral primary-research style: a concise multi-paragraph funnel, restrained first-person usage, no paper roadmap, and no result claims without verified data. Never assume that every Introduction must contain results, conclusions, or an organization paragraph.

### 2. Build an evidence ledger

For each planned factual or literature claim, record the claim, source, source type, and rhetorical purpose.

- Use reviews for established background concepts and field-level orientation.
- Use primary research for specific findings, data, comparisons, and limitations.
- Use authoritative organizational or governmental reports for official statistics.
- Prefer the original source over a secondary citation.
- Verify that the cited source actually supports the adjacent claim.

Never invent a citation, DOI, statistic, result, limitation, consensus, or gap. Mark unverifiable claims instead of making them sound certain.

### 3. Make a move map before prose

Organize the content as a general-to-specific funnel:

1. establish the territory and relevance;
2. synthesize pertinent prior research;
3. establish the niche through a verified gap, limitation, conflict, or need;
4. occupy the niche by stating what the present study does and why it matters.

Map every paragraph to one dominant function. Remove background that does not help the reader understand the gap or study. Keep the gap and present-study objective visibly connected, normally in the final one or two paragraphs.

### 4. Draft paragraph by paragraph

Begin each paragraph with a clear topic sentence. Move from broader concepts to the variables, system, population, setting, or mechanism investigated. Synthesize sources by theme or problem rather than listing authors one by one.

Use the sequence `known -> unresolved -> consequence/need -> present response`. Make the justification sentence explicit enough that a reader can answer, “Why is this study needed now?” State the objective with concrete verbs and enough scope to distinguish this study from prior work.

Include methods, hypotheses, principal findings, significance, contributions, or paper organization only when the article type and target venue support them.

### 5. Create cohesion without mechanical transitions

Maintain a controlled chain of key terms across sentences and paragraphs. Use demonstrative references only when the antecedent is unmistakable. Use adversative, causal, additive, and sequence connectors to express real logic; do not add them merely to make prose sound academic.

Combine concise sentences with longer evidence-rich sentences. Prefer clear verbs, short modifier chains, and accurate terminology. Imitate useful rhetorical patterns from strong papers but never copy whole sentences.

### 6. Control tense and strength of claim

Use present tense predominantly for accepted facts, definitions, and what the paper shows or addresses. Use past tense for completed procedures or individual past studies when reporting what was done; use present perfect when emphasizing a continuing body of research. Follow the target venue when conventions differ.

Match certainty to evidence. Distinguish `is`, `indicates`, `suggests`, `may`, and `remains unclear`. Do not convert an association into causation or a limited observation into a universal claim.

### 7. Audit and finalize

Apply the full rubric in [quality-checklist.md](references/quality-checklist.md). Confirm that:

- the opening establishes relevance without empty grand claims;
- the literature review is selective, current enough for the claim, and logically synthesized;
- the gap is supported rather than asserted;
- the objective directly answers the gap;
- contributions do not exceed the evidence;
- all citations and numbers are traceable;
- paragraph order, topic sentences, key-term chains, references, and transitions are coherent;
- the draft matches the target venue and requested length.

## Return a decision-ready result

For a full drafting or finalization request, return:

1. **Final Introduction** in the requested language and citation style;
2. **Move map** showing each paragraph's function, unless the user asks for prose only;
3. **Evidence flags** listing missing, weak, or unverified claims and citations;
4. **Key revision notes** limited to changes that affect logic, accuracy, or venue fit.

When editing an existing draft, preserve technical meaning and clearly flag any sentence whose correction requires domain evidence rather than stylistic judgment.
