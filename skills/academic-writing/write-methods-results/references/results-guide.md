# Results guide

## Purpose

Present the evidence that answers the study aims. Use prose to identify what matters, relate findings to the research question, and guide readers through figures and tables. Do not assume data "speak for themselves."

Results may stand alone or be combined with Discussion. Determine the journal convention before deciding how much interpretation and literature comparison belongs here.

## Flexible four-part model

Use these components selectively rather than mechanically:

1. **Orientation**: revisit the aim or a minimal methodological cue, preview the result, or direct the reader to a figure/table.
2. **Key findings**: report representative quantitative or qualitative results in sufficient detail.
3. **Comparisons**: compare groups, conditions, time points, model predictions, controls, or prior results when permitted.
4. **Problems or immediate implications**: mention anomalies, limitations, or tightly evidence-bound implications when they help readers interpret the finding and the journal permits them.

Not every paragraph needs all four components. Every Results section must present the key data and their relationship to the study aims.

## Build the Results map

Before writing, map each item:

| Research question or experiment | Figure/table | Brief method cue | Primary comparison | Numeric evidence | Supported takeaway |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Use this map to detect:

- results without a described method;
- methods with no reported result;
- figure/table order conflicts;
- duplicated claims;
- claims lacking numeric or qualitative evidence;
- inconsistent group names, units, sample sizes, or significance statements.

## Paragraph design

Use one main message per paragraph. A robust pattern is:

1. state the experiment's objective or bridge from the previous finding;
2. give only the methodological detail needed to understand the result;
3. identify the relevant figure/table naturally;
4. state the direction, magnitude, uncertainty, and statistical evidence of the key comparison;
5. end with a restrained summary or transition.

Vary location sentences:

- integrate the reference: "As shown in Figure 2, ...";
- put it parenthetically after the claim: "... increased after treatment (Figure 2).";
- use a separate location sentence only when it improves navigation.

Lead with the scientific finding instead of "Figure X shows" when the result is the point.

## Quantitative reporting

Combine three information types as needed:

1. **location**: where the evidence appears;
2. **technical description**: direction, magnitude, distribution, trend, relationship, or model fit;
3. **comment**: a narrow evidence-level statement explaining what the pattern establishes.

Report enough information to verify the claim:

- group or condition;
- sample size or replicate basis when appropriate;
- estimate and unit;
- variability or uncertainty;
- comparison and statistical test/evidence;
- exact or journal-compliant p-value, interval, effect size, or model metric when supplied.

Do not claim "significant" without clarifying whether it is statistical, practical, or scientific. Do not equate a non-significant result with proof of no effect. Do not infer causality from observational association.

Keep precision consistent with measurement and analysis. Never add decimal places not present in the source.

## Qualitative reporting

- Organize findings by theme, research question, or analytic category.
- State the theme, describe its evidentiary pattern, and use only necessary excerpts or examples.
- Preserve participant meaning and avoid turning prevalence language into unsupported counts.
- Distinguish direct evidence from analytic interpretation.

## Tables and figures

Choose the display according to the message:

- use a **table** for exact values, precision, detailed components, multidirectional comparisons, or calculations;
- use a **figure** for an overall trend, shape, sequence, relationship, or a small number of visually salient comparisons.

Do not duplicate the same complete dataset in prose and a display. In prose, identify the key pattern, exception, or comparison. Ensure captions and legends allow the display to be understood without reproducing the Results paragraph.

Follow current journal specifications for display formatting. Keep labels, units, legends, symbols, and panel references consistent with the text.

## Unexpected or problematic results

Report an anomaly when omitting it would mislead the reader or undermine later interpretation. Be candid and proportionate:

1. state the observation precisely;
2. delimit its scope;
3. identify a plausible measurement, sampling, equipment, or knowledge constraint only if supported;
4. note a sensitivity analysis or solution if one was actually performed.

Do not hide the problem, blame equipment without evidence, or use euphemism that changes the scientific meaning. Move broad limitation analysis and future work to Discussion unless the section is combined.

## Results versus Discussion boundary

Usually keep in Results:

- observed direction and magnitude;
- statistical or qualitative evidence;
- direct comparison with controls, groups, or model predictions;
- a brief statement of what the immediate pattern indicates.

Usually move to Discussion:

- biological, social, or physical mechanisms not directly tested;
- extensive comparison with literature;
- broad theoretical or practical implications;
- recommendations, generalization, limitations, and future work.

When the journal combines Results and Discussion, pair each result with a clearly marked interpretation and literature context without repeating the numeric result later.

## Common failure patterns

Reject or repair Results prose that:

- follows raw analysis order instead of the research aims or figure/table sequence;
- repeats every number or restates the caption;
- uses "increased," "better," or "correlated" without a baseline, direction, magnitude, or evidence;
- introduces a method absent from Methods;
- reports inconsistent sample sizes, labels, units, or p-values;
- treats p-values as effect sizes or uses causal language unsupported by design;
- buries the primary result after secondary details;
- repeats Results as Discussion or inserts speculative mechanisms.
